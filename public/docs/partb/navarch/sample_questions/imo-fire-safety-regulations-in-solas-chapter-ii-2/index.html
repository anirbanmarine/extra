      IMO Fire Safety Regulations-Layers of Protection & SOLAS Chapter II-2

      IMO Fire Safety Regulations-Layers of Protection & SOLAS Chapter II-2

      1. Layers of Protection in Fire Safety (IMO Approach)

      The IMO’s fire safety philosophy is based on four layers of protection, designed to address fire at every stage:

      Layer Objective Key Measures (SOLAS Ch. II-2)
      Prevention Minimize the probability of fire ignition. - Restrictions on ignition sources (e.g., electrical equipment, hot work permits).- Use of non-combustible materials in construction.- Smoking restrictions in cargo holds and accommodation spaces.
      Fire Spread Prevention Limit the spread of fire if ignition occurs. - Structural fire divisions (A, B, C class bulkheads/decks).- Fire doors (self-closing, fire-rated).- Fire dampers in ventilation systems.- Thermal insulation to delay heat transfer.
      Fire Extinction Detect and extinguish fires effectively. - Fixed fire extinguishing systems (CO₂, foam, water mist).- Portable extinguishers (dry chemical, foam, CO₂).- Fire main system (hose reels, hydrants).- Automatic sprinklers in accommodation spaces.
      Safety of Life Ensure safe evacuation and survival of crew and passengers. - Emergency escape routes (protected, illuminated).- Firefighter’s outfits and breathing apparatus.- Lifesaving appliances (lifeboats, liferafts).- Fire drills and training.

      2. Fire Safety Objectives, Functional Requirements & SOLAS Ch. II-2

      A. Fire Safety Objectives (IMO)

      The primary objectives of IMO’s fire safety regulations are:

      1. Prevent fire occurrence (minimize ignition sources).
      2. Limit fire growth (contain fire to the compartment of origin).
      3. Ensure structural integrity (prevent collapse due to fire).
      4. Facilitate evacuation (safe escape routes).
      5. Enable fire suppression (effective extinguishing systems).
      6. Protect adjacent spaces (prevent fire spread).

      B. Functional Requirements (SOLAS Ch. II-2)

      SOLAS Chapter II-2 (Fire Protection, Fire Detection, and Fire Extinction) is performance-based, meaning it specifies what must be achieved rather than how to achieve it. The functional requirements include:

      Functional Requirement Purpose Example Regulations in SOLAS Ch. II-2
      Containment of Fire Fire must be contained within the compartment of origin for a specified time. - A-60, B-15, C-0 class divisions (Reg. 9).- Fire doors (Reg. 9.4).- Fire dampers (Reg. 9.7).
      Limitation of Fire Spread Prevent fire from spreading to adjacent compartments. - Structural fire integrity (Reg. 9).- Thermal insulation (Reg. 9.2).- Non-combustible materials (Reg. 3).
      Extinction of Fire Provide means to detect and extinguish fires. - Fixed fire extinguishing systems (Reg. 10).- Portable extinguishers (Reg. 10.2).- Fire main system (Reg. 10.1).
      Protection of Escape Routes Ensure safe evacuation in case of fire. - Protected escape routes (Reg. 13).- Emergency lighting (Reg. 13.3).- Low-location lighting (Reg. 13.3.2).
      Structural Integrity Maintain ship’s structural strength during and after a fire. - Fire resistance ratings (A-60, B-15, etc.).- Load-bearing capacity (Reg. 9.2.2).

      3. Specialty of SOLAS Chapter II-2 Arrangement

      SOLAS Chapter II-2 is unique because it:

      1. Adopts a Performance-Based Approach:
        • Specifies objectives and functional requirements rather than prescriptive solutions.
        • Allows innovative designs (e.g., alternative fire suppression systems) if they meet the same safety level.
      2. Incorporates Risk-Based Design:
        • Fire Risk Assessment (FRA) can be used to justify alternative arrangements (Reg. 17).
        • Encourages design improvements (e.g., safe return to port concept).
      3. Covers All Ship Types:
        • Applies to passenger ships, cargo ships, and tankers with specific amendments for each.
      4. Regularly Updated:
        • Amendments incorporate lessons from incidents (e.g., Scandinavian Star, Emma Maersk).

      4. Structural Fire Divisions on a Ship

      A. Types of Fire Divisions (SOLAS Reg. 9)

      Structural fire divisions are classified based on their fire resistance and ability to prevent fire spread. The three main classes are:

      Class Fire Resistance Rating Purpose Typical Applications
      A-0 0 minutes No fire resistance (used for non-critical spaces). Store rooms, non-essential spaces.
      A-15 15 minutes Low fire resistance (delays fire spread). Corridors, stairways (in some cases).
      A-30 30 minutes Moderate fire resistance. Accommodation bulkheads, some cargo holds.
      A-60 60 minutes High fire resistance (prevents fire spread for 60 mins). Primary fire divisions (e.g., between cargo holds and accommodation).
      B-0 0 minutes Non-combustible materials but no insulation. External bulkheads (non-load-bearing).
      B-15 15 minutes Non-combustible + insulation (delays temperature rise). Some machinery spaces.
      C Non-combustible No fire resistance rating but non-combustible (e.g., steel). Decks in open areas, non-fire-risk spaces.

      B. Purpose of Structural Fire Divisions

      1. Contain Fire: Prevent fire from spreading beyond the compartment of origin.
      2. Delay Fire Spread: Provide time for evacuation and firefighting.
      3. Protect Critical Spaces: Shield accommodation, control stations, and escape routes.
      4. Maintain Structural Integrity: Ensure the ship does not collapse during a fire.

      5. Fire Bulkheads and Decks: Testing Methods

      A. Standard Fire Test (SOLAS Reg. 3.2)

      The standard fire test evaluates the fire resistance of bulkheads and decks. It involves:

      1. Exposure to a Standard Fire Curve:
        • A time-temperature curve simulating a fully developed fire.
        • Temperature rises according to the formula: T = 345 log₁₀(8t + 1), where t = time in minutes.
      2. Criteria for Passing:
        • Integrity: No flames or hot gases pass through.
        • Insulation: Temperature rise on the unexposed side does not exceed:
          • 140°C above ambient (average).
          • 180°C above ambient (at any point).
        • Load-bearing capacity: Must withstand mechanical stress during the test.

      B. Non-Combustibility Test (SOLAS Reg. 3.1)

      • Purpose: Ensure materials do not contribute to fire spread.
      • Test Method (ISO 1182):
        • A small sample is exposed to 750°C for 5 minutes in a furnace.
        • Passing Criteria:
          • No flame propagation.
          • Temperature rise ≤ 30°C in the furnace.
          • Weight loss ≤ 50% of the original sample.

      B. Significance of the Standard Fire Curve

      • Simulates a fully developed fire in a compartment.
      • Used to test fire resistance of bulkheads, decks, and doors.
      • Ensures consistent testing worldwide (IMO standardized).
      • Real-world relevance: Mimics cellulosic fires (e.g., wood, paper, textiles).

      7. Case Study: Scandinavian Star (1990)

      A. Incident Overview

      • Date: April 7, 1990.
      • Ship: Scandinavian Star (passenger/vehicle ferry).
      • Casualties: 159 deaths (one of the deadliest maritime disasters in peacetime).
      • Cause: Fire originated in a car deck, spread rapidly due to:
        • Inadequate fire divisions (B-class bulkheads instead of A-class).
        • Poor fire detection (no sprinklers in car decks).
        • Delayed evacuation (smoke spread to accommodation via ventilation systems).
        • Failure of fire doors (not self-closing).

      B. Lessons Learned & SOLAS Amendments

      1. Stricter Fire Division Requirements:
        • Car decks now require A-60 class divisions (previously B-0 or B-15).
      2. Improved Fire Detection:
        • Automatic sprinklers mandatory in car decks and special category spaces (Reg. 10.2.1.3).
      3. Enhanced Ventilation Control:
        • Fire dampers must automatically close in case of fire (Reg. 9.7).
      4. Better Escape Routes:
        • Protected stairways and low-location lighting (Reg. 13).
      5. Human Element:
        • Mandatory fire drills and crew training (SOLAS Ch. II-2/15).

      8. Significance of Insulation and Cable Penetrations in Fire Bulkheads/Decks

      A. Insulation

      • Purpose:
        • Delays heat transfer to the unexposed side of the bulkhead/deck.
        • Prevents fire spread by maintaining temperature rise limits (140°C avg, 180°C max).
      • Materials:
        • Mineral wool, ceramic fiber, or calcium silicate (non-combustible).
      • Case Study: Emma Maersk (2006)
        • Issue: Fire in engine room spread via unprotected cable penetrations.
        • Lesson: Cable penetrations must be sealed with fire-resistant materials (e.g., fire-stopping systems).
        • SOLAS Requirement: Reg. 9.2.3 mandates fire-resistant sealing for all penetrations.

      B. Cable Penetrations

      • Risk: Fire can spread through gaps where cables pass through bulkheads/decks.
      • Solution:
        • Fire-stopping systems (e.g., intumescent seals, fire-resistant putty).
        • Tested to the same fire resistance as the bulkhead (e.g., A-60 penetration = A-60 seal).
      • Emma Maersk Incident (2006):
        • Fire spread from the engine room to accommodation via unprotected cable penetrations.
        • Result: SOLAS amendments now require strict testing of cable transits (Reg. 9.2.3.2).

      9. Design Innovations in Fire Safety

      Modern ships incorporate innovative fire safety designs, such as:

      Innovation Description Example/SOLAS Reference
      Safe Return to Port (SRtP) Ships designed to return to port safely after a fire incident. SOLAS II-2/21 (for passenger ships).
      Water Mist Systems Fine water spray extinguishes fire by cooling and oxygen displacement. Reg. 10.4 (allowed as an alternative to CO₂ in some spaces).
      Fire-Resistant Composite Materials Lightweight, non-combustible materials (e.g., aluminum foam, fire-rated composites). Reg. 3 (non-combustibility requirements).
      Advanced Fire Detection (AI/ML) AI-based fire detection (e.g., thermal cameras, smoke analysis). Reg. 7 (fire detection systems).
      Modular Fireproofing Prefabricated fire-resistant panels for easier installation and maintenance. Used in modern cruise ships and LNG carriers.
      Fire-Resistant Coatings Intumescent paints expand when exposed to heat, insulating steel structures. Reg. 9.2 (structural fire protection).

      10. New Sections of SOLAS Chapter II-2

      A. Design Improvements (Allowing for Innovation)

      SOLAS Ch. II-2 includes new parts that allow for design improvements:

      Part Focus Key Regulation
      Part F Alternative Designs and Arrangements Reg. 17: Allows Fire Risk Assessment (FRA) to justify alternative designs.
      Part G Special Requirements for Passenger Ships Reg. 18-20: Safe Return to Port (SRtP) and evacuation analysis.
      Part H Fire Safety for Ships Carrying Dangerous Goods Reg. 19: Additional protections for LNG, LPG, and chemical tankers.
      Part J Fire Safety Measures for Ships Using Low-Flashpoint Fuels Reg. 21: Addresses LNG-fueled ships and methane slip risks.

      B. Human Element (Crew and Passenger Safety)

      SOLAS Ch. II-2 also addresses the human element in fire safety:

      Aspect SOLAS Regulation Purpose
      Fire Drills Reg. 15.2.2 Mandatory weekly drills for crew.
      Training Reg. 15.2.1 Firefighting training for all crew members.
      Evacuation Plans Reg. 13.7 Clear escape route signage and muster station assignments.
      Firefighter’s Outfits Reg. 10.10 Personal protective equipment (PPE) for firefighting teams.
      Communication Reg. 10.6 Fire alarm systems with audible and visual signals.

      11. Key Takeaways

      1. Layers of Protection: IMO’s approach ensures prevention, containment, extinction, and life safety.
      2. SOLAS Ch. II-2: Performance-based, allowing innovation while maintaining safety.
      3. Structural Fire Divisions: A-60, B-15, C-0 classifications ensure fire containment.
      4. Testing: Standard fire test and non-combustibility test validate fire resistance.
      5. Case Studies: Scandinavian Star (fire spread due to poor divisions) and Emma Maersk (cable penetrations) led to SOLAS amendments.
      6. Human Element: Training, drills, and evacuation plans are critical for fire safety.
      7. Design Innovations: Water mist, AI detection, and fire-resistant materials improve modern ship safety.
