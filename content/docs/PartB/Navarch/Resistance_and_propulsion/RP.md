## 1. Powering chain

Effective power is the power needed to tow the bare hull at a specified speed, without propeller action.

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    A[Engine] --> B[BHP]
    B --> C[Reduction gear]
    C --> D[Shaft power SP]
    D --> E[Shafting bearings and seals]
    E --> F[Delivered power DP]
    F --> G[Propeller]
    G --> H[Thrust power TP]
    H --> I[Hull-propeller interaction]
    I --> J[Effective power EP]
```

The normal relationship is \(BHP > SP > DP > TP > EP\), because power is lost through gears, bearings, seals, propeller action, friction, heat, vibration and sound.

- **Brake horsepower (BHP):** Engine output before the reduction gear.
- **Shaft power (SP):** Power available after the reduction gear.
- **Delivered power (DP):** Power delivered to the propeller.
- **Thrust power (TP):** Power generated by propeller thrust.
- **Effective power (EP):** Power required to overcome total hull resistance.

## 2. Power and efficiency formulas

Effective power is

\(\mathrm{EP}=R_TV_S\)

where \(R_T\) is total resistance in newtons and \(V_S\) is ship speed in metres per second.

The principal efficiencies are:

- Gear efficiency: \(\eta_{\mathrm{gear}}=\frac{SP}{BHP}\)
- Shaft transmission efficiency: \(\eta_{\mathrm{shaft}}=\frac{DP}{SP}\)
- Propeller efficiency: \(\eta_{\mathrm{prop}}=\frac{TP}{DP}\)
- Hull efficiency: \(\eta_H=\frac{EP}{TP}\)
- Overall propulsive efficiency: \(\eta_P=\frac{EP}{SP}\)

Therefore,

\(\eta_P=\eta_{\mathrm{shaft}}\eta_{\mathrm{prop}}\eta_H\)

and

\(EP=SP\eta_P\)

Thus, the required shaft power is

\(SP=\frac{EP}{\eta_P}\)

The overall power relationship is

\(EP=BHP\eta_{\mathrm{gear}}\eta_{\mathrm{shaft}}\eta_{\mathrm{prop}}\eta_H\)

Typical approximate values are:

| Efficiency | Typical value |
|---|---:|
| Gear efficiency | \(98\%-99\%\) |
| Shaft efficiency | \(97\%-98\%\) |
| Propeller efficiency | \(70\%-75\%\) |
| Overall propulsive efficiency | \(55\%-75\%\) |

**Example:** If \(EP=23{,}500\ \mathrm{HP}\) and \(\eta_P=0.68\),

\(SP=\frac{23{,}500}{0.68}=34{,}559\ \mathrm{HP}\)

## 3. Total hull resistance

Total hull resistance is the force opposing ship motion.

The basic resistance components are

\(R_T=R_{AA}+R_W+R_V\)

where:

- \(R_{AA}\): Calm-air resistance on the above-water structure.
- \(R_W\): Wave-making resistance.
- \(R_V\): Viscous resistance, including frictional and form resistance.

Additional resistances may include appendage resistance, steering resistance, added resistance in waves and shallow-water resistance.

### Main concepts

- At low speed, viscous resistance generally dominates.
- At higher speed, wave-making resistance rises rapidly.
- Air resistance is commonly about \(4\%-8\%\) of total resistance.
- Appendage resistance may be significant, especially in naval vessels.
- Fouling, corrosion and rough paint increase resistance.
- Shallow water increases flow velocity beneath the hull, pressure changes, wave-making resistance and frictional resistance.

## 4. Resistance coefficients

The coefficient of total resistance is

\(C_T=\frac{R_T}{\frac{1}{2}\rho S V_S^2}\)

Therefore,

\(R_T=C_T\left(\frac{1}{2}\rho S V_S^2\right)\)

where:

- \(\rho\): Water density.
- \(S\): Wetted surface area.
- \(V_S\): Ship speed.

The total coefficient may be expressed as

\(C_T=C_V+C_W+C_A\)

or, when correlation allowance is included,

\(C_T=C_V+C_W+C_A+C_{A,\mathrm{corr}}\)

where:

- \(C_V\): Viscous-resistance coefficient.
- \(C_W\): Wave-making-resistance coefficient.
- \(C_A\): Air-resistance coefficient.
- \(C_{A,\mathrm{corr}}\): Correlation allowance for full-scale effects.

## 5. Viscous resistance

Viscous resistance results from water viscosity and pressure changes around the hull.

The viscous coefficient is

\(C_V=C_F+KC_F=(1+K)C_F\)

where:

- \(C_F\): Skin-friction coefficient.
- \(K\): Form factor.
- \(KC_F\): Viscous pressure or form-drag component.

The Reynolds number is

\(Re=\frac{V_SL}{\nu}\)

where \(L\) is ship length and \(\nu\) is kinematic viscosity.

A commonly used ITTC skin-friction relation is

\(C_F=\frac{0.075}{(\log_{10}Re-2)^2}\)

Approximate flow regimes over a flat plate are:

- Laminar flow: \(Re<5\times10^5\).
- Turbulent flow: \(Re>5\times10^5\).

### Form factor

A fuller hull generally has a greater form factor and produces more pressure drag. A slender hull reduces pressure differences and eddy formation.

One empirical form-factor expression given in the notes is

\(K=19\left(\frac{B}{L}\right)^2\left(\frac{T}{B}\right)^{0.5}\left(\frac{\nabla}{L^3}\right)^{0.5}\)

where \(B\) is beam, \(T\) is draft and \(\nabla\) is displacement volume.

### Boundary-layer separation

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    A[Flow along hull] --> B[Boundary layer develops]
    B --> C[Adverse pressure gradient]
    C --> D[Boundary-layer separation]
    D --> E[Wake and eddies]
    E --> F[Viscous pressure/form drag]
```

Boundary-layer separation causes a large wake and increases pressure drag. It can be reduced by using a slender, smoothly faired hull.

## 6. Froude number and wave resistance

The Froude number compares inertia forces with gravity forces:

\(Fn=\frac{V_S}{\sqrt{gL}}\)

where \(g\) is gravitational acceleration and \(L\) is characteristic ship length.

The speed-to-length ratio is

\(\frac{V_S}{\sqrt{L}}\)

but it is not dimensionless.

Wave resistance depends mainly on:

- Froude number.
- Beam-to-length ratio.
- Displacement.
- Hull shape.
- Ship length and speed.

Ship-generated waves consist mainly of:

- Transverse waves.
- Divergent waves from the bow and stern.

The approximate wavelength of the generated wave is

\(\lambda_W=\frac{2\pi V_S^2}{g}\)

The approximate hull-speed relationship is

\(V_H=1.34\sqrt{L_{\mathrm{ft}}}\ \mathrm{knots}\)

At hull speed, the transverse wavelength becomes approximately equal to ship length. Wave-making resistance then increases sharply.

- A **hump** occurs when bow and stern waves reinforce each other.
- A **hollow** occurs when they partially cancel each other.
- Increasing ship length raises hull speed.
- A bulbous bow can reduce bow-wave resistance through wave interaction.

## 7. Model testing and scaling

A towing-tank model requires geometric and dynamic similarity.

The scale factor is

\(\lambda=\frac{L_S}{L_M}\)

where subscripts \(S\) and \(M\) denote ship and model.

Scaling relationships are:

- Length: \(L_S=\lambda L_M\)
- Area: \(S_S=\lambda^2S_M\)
- Volume or displacement: \(\nabla_S=\lambda^3\nabla_M\)

For corresponding speeds, Froude’s law gives

\(\frac{V_S}{\sqrt{L_S}}=\frac{V_M}{\sqrt{L_M}}\)

Therefore,

\(V_M=\frac{V_S}{\sqrt{\lambda}}\)

and

\(V_S=V_M\sqrt{\lambda}\)

Use SI units for velocity:

\(1\ \mathrm{knot}=0.5144\ \mathrm{m/s}\)

For dynamically similar testing, the wave-resistance coefficient is transferred as

\(C_{W,M}=C_{W,S}\)

The model total-resistance coefficient is

\(C_{T,M}=\frac{R_{T,M}}{\frac{1}{2}\rho_M S_MV_M^2}\)

Hence,

\(C_{W,M}=C_{T,M}-C_{V,M}\)

For the full-scale ship,

\(C_{T,S}=C_{V,S}+C_{W,S}+C_{A,S}+C_{A,\mathrm{corr}}\)

Then,

\(R_{T,S}=C_{T,S}\left(\frac{1}{2}\rho_SS_SV_S^2\right)\)

Finally,

\(EP_S=R_{T,S}V_S\)

## 8. Propeller terminology

```mermaid
flowchart LR
    A[Engine rotation] --> B[Propeller rotation]
    B --> C[Pressure difference over blades]
    C --> D[Water accelerated astern]
    D --> E[Forward thrust]
    E --> F[Ship advances]
```

Important parts and terms:

- **Diameter \(D\):** Tip-to-tip distance.
- **Hub:** Central connection to the shaft.
- **Blade root:** Blade connection to the hub.
- **Blade tip:** Outermost part of the blade.
- **Leading edge:** First edge to meet incoming flow.
- **Trailing edge:** Rear edge of the blade.
- **Pressure face:** High-pressure side.
- **Suction back:** Low-pressure side.
- **Pitch \(P\):** Theoretical forward distance per revolution in an ideal fluid.
- **Propeller disc:** Area swept by the blade tips.
- **Projected area \(A_P\):** Blade area projected perpendicular to the shaft.
- **Developed area:** Actual blade driving-face area.
- **Blade-area ratio:** \(BAR=\frac{\text{developed blade area excluding boss}}{\text{propeller disc area}}\)
- **Disc-area ratio:** \(DAR=\frac{\text{developed blade area including boss}}{\text{propeller disc area}}\)

The pitch ratio is

\(P/D=\frac{\text{pitch}}{\text{diameter}}\)

Theoretical propeller speed is

\(V_T=PN\)

when \(P\) is in metres per revolution and \(N\) is in revolutions per second.

The propeller pitch angle is

\(\tan\phi=\frac{P}{2\pi r}\)

Types of pitch include:

- **Fixed pitch:** Blade pitch cannot be altered.
- **Constant pitch:** Pitch remains constant along the blade radius.
- **Variable pitch:** Pitch changes along the blade radius.
- **Controllable pitch:** Blade angle can be changed during operation.

## 9. Wake, advance speed and slip

The propeller operates in the wake produced by the ship.

Wake fraction is

\(w=\frac{V_S-V_A}{V_S}\)

Therefore, speed of advance is

\(V_A=V_S(1-w)\)

Approximate wake fraction:

\(w\approx0.5C_B-0.05\)

where \(C_B\) is the block coefficient.

The theoretical propeller speed is

\(V_T=PN\)

Apparent slip is

\(s_A=\frac{V_T-V_S}{V_T}\)

Real slip is

\(s_R=\frac{V_T-V_A}{V_T}\)

Real slip is independent of current, whereas apparent slip is based on the ship’s speed through the water.

## 10. Thrust and propeller performance

For an idealised momentum model, the mass flow through the propeller disc per second is

\(\dot m=\rho A_PPN\)

The increase in water velocity is

\(\Delta V=PN-V_A\)

The thrust is

\(T=\rho A_PPN(PN-V_A)\)

Alternatively, if real slip \(s\) is defined by \(PN-V_A=sPN\),

\(T=\rho A_P^2N^2s\)

Thrust power is

\(TP=TV_A\)

Propeller efficiency is

\(\eta_{\mathrm{prop}}=\frac{TP}{DP}=\frac{TV_A}{2\pi NQ}\)

where \(Q\) is propeller torque.

The thrust coefficient is

\(K_T=\frac{T}{\rho n^2D^4}\)

where \(n\) is revolutions per second.

The thrust-loading coefficient is

\(C_T=\frac{T}{\frac{1}{2}\rho A_OD^2?}\)

Use the notation consistently with the propeller reference: the propeller disc area is \(A_O\), and the loading coefficient is commonly written as

\(C_T=\frac{T}{\frac{1}{2}\rho A_OV_A^2}\)

A larger propeller diameter generally improves efficiency for a given thrust because it reduces disc loading, subject to draft, clearance and stern-form limitations.

## 11. Cavitation and ventilation

**Cavitation** occurs when local pressure on a propeller blade falls below the vapour pressure of water, producing vapour bubbles that subsequently collapse.

Effects include:

- Reduced propeller efficiency.
- Loss of thrust.
- Blade erosion.
- Vibration.
- Noise.
- Possible structural damage.

Cavitation becomes more likely when:

- Propeller thrust loading is high.
- RPM changes rapidly.
- Propeller diameter is too small.
- Blade surfaces are fouled, nicked or rough.
- Water pressure is low.

It can be reduced by:

- Increasing propeller diameter where practicable.
- Reducing thrust loading.
- Selecting suitable RPM and pitch.
- Maintaining smooth, clean blades.
- Changing RPM gradually.
- Operating submerged propellers at greater depth when possible.

**Ventilation** occurs when air or exhaust gas is drawn into a propeller operating close to the free surface. It reduces the water loading on the blades and produces effects similar to cavitation.

## 12. Practical resistance-reduction measures

- Keep the underwater hull clean and smooth.
- Prevent corrosion and marine fouling.
- Use an efficient, slender hull form.
- Operate below the resistance-hump speed where practical.
- Use a properly designed bulbous bow.
- Optimise propeller diameter, pitch and RPM.
- Minimise unnecessary appendages.
- Consider shallow-water effects when selecting operating speed.
