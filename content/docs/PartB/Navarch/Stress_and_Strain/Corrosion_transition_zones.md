# Q.4 — Corrosion as a Prime Cause of Oil Tanker Structural Failures: Casualty Analysis and Risk at Ballast Tank Boundaries and Transition Zones

**The general finding from casualty investigations**

Since the early 1990s, a series of major tanker structural failures — several resulting in the vessel breaking in two and catastrophic oil pollution — have been investigated by flag states, classification societies, and IMO, and in almost every case, **uncontrolled/undetected corrosion wastage of primary structural members was identified as a principal contributing cause**, often acting in combination with fatigue cracking, inadequate survey/maintenance history, and in some cases heavy weather loading that a sound structure would have withstood.

**Illustrative casualties**

1. **MV Nakhodka (1997)** — A Russian-flagged tanker that broke in two in the Sea of Japan during heavy weather, spilling a large quantity of heavy fuel oil. Post-casualty investigation attributed the failure substantially to **severe corrosion of the deck and side shell structure combined with fatigue cracking** at a location that had not been adequately assessed in prior surveys, illustrating how wastage in way of critical transverse structure reduces the hull girder's residual strength below what heavy weather bending moments then exceeded.

2. **Erika (1999)** — A tanker that broke in two off the coast of Brittany, France, causing a major pollution incident along the French coastline. The official investigation found that the hull had suffered from **extensive corrosion in the ballast tanks**, particularly in way of the side shell frames and brackets, which had progressively weakened the transverse structure over a period during which surveys and repairs had not adequately addressed the deterioration. The incident was a major driver behind the EU's subsequent tightening of tanker safety and inspection regimes (the "Erika packages").

3. **Prestige (2002)** — A single-hull tanker that suffered structural failure off the coast of Spain in heavy weather, ultimately breaking apart and sinking with a very large quantity of fuel oil, causing extensive pollution along the Spanish and French coasts. Structural assessment pointed to **corrosion-weakened side shell plating and internal structure**, with the initiating crack/failure occurring in a heavily corroded area of the hull that had lost significant section modulus, again in combination with severe weather bending loads.

4. **Kirki (1991)** and several other 1980s–90s single-hull tanker losses were similarly traced back to corrosion-driven loss of the bow structure or hull girder strength, reinforcing the pattern well before Erika and Prestige brought it to wider public/regulatory attention.

**Why the risk of failure is elevated specifically adjacent to ballast tanks**

- **One-sided exposure and asymmetric corrosion environment**: A structural member forming the common boundary between a cargo oil tank and a ballast tank (a longitudinal bulkhead, for example) experiences **corrosive attack from both faces simultaneously, but from two different aggressive environments** — the cargo side subject to acid condensation/H₂S attack as in Q.1, and the ballast side subject to seawater corrosion, particularly aggressive when ballast is carried in tanks that are only intermittently coated-maintained or where coating breakdown allows seawater contact with bare steel for extended periods.
- **Ballast tanks are wet for extended periods and experience the classic seawater corrosion cycle** — full immersion, wet/dry cycling at the waterline as ballast is taken/discharged, and residual moisture even when "empty," all of which sustain a continuously active corrosion cell on the ballast side, unlike cargo tanks where the liquid, while acidic in the vapour space, is oil rather than an electrolyte over most of the wetted surface.
- **Coating deterioration is typically most advanced in ballast tanks** because of the combination of mechanical damage (ballast water turbulence, anchor chain/anodes chafing), oxygenated seawater, and historically shorter coating service lives before PSPC — meaning the ballast-side face of these common structural members frequently reaches bare-steel condition well before the cargo-side face does, making these members net thinner from both sides at once.
- Consequently, **double-bottom structure (inner bottom plating, bottom longitudinals, floors) and the double-hull side structure at the cargo/ballast boundary consistently show up in survey data and casualty records as having the highest wastage rates** in the ship, precisely because they sit at this two-sided corrosive interface.

**Why transition zones are especially vulnerable**

"Transition zones" refers to locations where there is an **abrupt change in structural continuity, geometry, or material** — for example:
- Where the double bottom structure meets the sloped hopper tank/bilge structure
- Where a longitudinal bulkhead terminates or changes in stiffening arrangement
- Where scantlings change from one strength grade of steel to another, or where plate thickness steps down
- At the ends of the cargo tank block, where the more heavily framed engine room/forecastle structure meets the more lightly framed cargo tank structure
- At bracket toes, knuckle points, and openings (manholes, access hatches) in primary members

These zones are disproportionately at risk for several combined reasons:
1. **Stress concentration**: Any geometric discontinuity — a knuckle, bracket toe, opening, or abrupt scantling change — inherently produces **localized stress concentration** under the hull girder's global bending and local pressure loads, well above the nominal/average stress level elsewhere in the member. Corrosion wastage at exactly these high-stress points reduces section modulus precisely where the margin against yield/fatigue failure is already thinnest.
2. **Coating application difficulty**: Complex geometry at brackets, knuckles, and structural transitions is notoriously **difficult to coat effectively during original construction** — spray access is restricted, and edges/corners are prone to low dry-film thickness — meaning these areas are statistically more likely to suffer early coating breakdown and hence earlier corrosion onset than flat, easily-coated plate surfaces (this links directly to Q.5's discussion of initial coating difficulty).
3. **Fatigue crack initiation sites**: Because these are also the classic locations for fatigue crack initiation (see Q.6), corrosion and fatigue **interact synergistically** — corrosion pitting can act as a stress-raiser that initiates a fatigue crack earlier than would occur on smooth, uncorroded plate, and a fatigue crack, once initiated, provides a fresh crevice that further localizes and accelerates corrosion attack. This corrosion-fatigue interaction was specifically identified in several of the above casualty investigations as compounding the loss of structural integrity faster than either mechanism would alone.
4. **Cumulative effect on hull girder strength**: Because the double bottom and deck are the primary tension/compression flanges of the hull girder box-section, and transition zones/ballast-tank-adjacent members contribute significantly to their combined section modulus, **corrosion-driven thickness loss concentrated at these specific locations reduces overall hull girder strength disproportionately** relative to the same percentage wastage occurring more generally across less critical areas — which is exactly the mechanism identified as leading to the sudden brittle-style overload failures seen in the Erika, Prestige, and Nakhodka casualties.

**Summary of the causal chain identified across these casualties**
Undetected/underestimated corrosion wastage (often worse than average survey figures suggested, due to the pitting/localization issues in Q.2) concentrated at ballast-tank-adjacent structure and transition zones → reduced local section modulus and increased stress concentration at points already vulnerable to fatigue → crack initiation and propagation → combined with heavy weather bending moment → residual hull girder strength falls below the applied load → catastrophic hull girder failure (breaking in two), with major oil outflow as the consequence.
