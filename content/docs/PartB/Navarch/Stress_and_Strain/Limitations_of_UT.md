# Q.7 — Limitations of Structural Surveys, Inspections, and Thickness Measurements in Assessing Corrosion and Diminution

While the Enhanced Survey Programme (ESP), close-up surveys, and thickness measurement (TM) regimes form the backbone of tanker structural assessment, each method carries inherent limitations that mean the reported condition of a ship's structure is, at best, a **statistically incomplete and time-lagged approximation** of true condition — not an exhaustive, real-time picture. These limitations must be understood by anyone relying on survey/TM reports to judge actual structural safety margins.

## 1. Sampling Nature of Thickness Measurement — Spot Readings Cannot Capture Localized Attack

- UT thickness gauging is inherently a **point-sampling technique**: a probe is placed at a discrete location, and the reading represents the thickness at that exact spot only, typically a few square centimetres.
- As discussed in Q.2, **pitting corrosion is highly localized** — a pit only a few millimetres in diameter can have penetrated to near-perforation while a TM reading taken even 50–100 mm away shows apparently sound plate. Class society TM patterns (typically a grid of points per panel, per IACS UR Z10 series requirements) are a **statistical sample**, not a full-coverage survey, and by definition cannot guarantee detection of every pit, particularly where pit density is low but individual pit depth is severe.
- Reported "average diminution" or "percentage wastage" figures derived from TM readings can therefore **mask the true worst-case condition** of a panel — a plate can show acceptable average thickness while containing isolated pits at or beyond the renewal/critical threshold.

## 2. Accessibility Constraints Limit Coverage

- Many critical structural locations — the underside of deck structure in way of complex framing, the interior of box girders, the crown of hopper tanks, areas behind permanent staging, pipe runs, or ladders — are **physically difficult or impossible to reach** for direct contact UT measurement without extensive staging, rafting, or confined-space access, which is costly and time-limited during a survey/dry-dock window.
- Where access is difficult, surveyors and owners may rely on either **reduced sampling density** in these areas or on remote means (see point 5) that have their own accuracy limitations, meaning the most geometrically complex — and, as discussed in Q.4 and Q.6, often the most corrosion-and-fatigue-prone — areas are frequently the least thoroughly measured.
- Confined space entry requirements (gas-freeing, ventilation, permits) further restrict the time available inside a tank during a survey, incentivizing surveyors toward efficient sampling patterns rather than exhaustive coverage.

## 3. Coating Presence Obscures the Underlying Steel Condition

- Where a tank retains an intact coating (which is the desired condition and is itself credited during survey), the coating **conceals the true condition of the steel beneath it** from ordinary visual inspection — a surveyor can assess coating condition (breakdown percentage, rust staining, blistering) but cannot visually confirm actual steel thickness or the presence of pitting occurring under an apparently intact but disbonded coating film ("under-film corrosion").
- This means visual survey findings of "coating GOOD, no action" can coexist with **underlying, undetected pitting or general wastage** that has begun beneath localized coating disbondment not yet visible from the surface, particularly relevant at the vapour space/deckhead areas of Q.1.

## 4. Survey is a Point-in-Time Snapshot, Not a Continuous Record

- A TM survey or close-up survey reports condition **only at the moment of inspection**, typically conducted at intervals of 2.5–5 years for ESP-applicable tankers depending on age.
- Corrosion (particularly pitting, which can be autocatalytic and accelerating as discussed in Q.2) and fatigue crack growth are **continuous, time-dependent processes** — the true condition of the structure at any point between two surveys is unknown and can only be extrapolated (often linearly, which may not reflect the true accelerating/decelerating nature of the actual degradation mechanism).
- A structure assessed as satisfactory at one survey can deteriorate to a critical condition before the next scheduled survey, particularly if an unanticipated event (coating mechanical damage, cargo change, unusually corrosive cargo/ballast conditions) occurs in the intervening period — the survey regime has **no inherent capability to detect or respond to such intervening deterioration** except through the ship's own routine (non-class) inspection and reporting, which is of variable quality depending on crew diligence and training.

## 5. Limitations of the Measurement Technology Itself

- **Conventional UT gauging** measures thickness accurately at a clean, accessible, reasonably flat spot but is sensitive to surface preparation — loose scale, marine growth, or paint over the measurement point can produce erroneous readings if not properly prepared before gauging.
- UT does not reliably distinguish between **general wastage and the shape/depth profile of a pit** unless a dedicated pit-gauge or a much denser measurement grid is specifically used at a location already suspected of pitting — routine survey grids are typically not dense enough to fully characterize pit geometry.
- **Automated/remote techniques** (robotic crawlers, drone-based visual inspection now increasingly accepted by class societies as alternative survey methods) improve coverage in difficult-to-access areas but introduce their own limitations — drone visual inspection cannot measure thickness at all (visual only), and robotic UT crawlers may have limited ability to negotiate complex stiffened geometry, weld seams, and corners — precisely the transition-zone/bracket-toe areas of greatest concern from Q.4 and Q.6.

## 6. Crack Detection Limitations

- **Visual examination**, the primary means of crack detection during survey, can only detect cracks that are already large enough to be visible to the eye, meaning by definition **early-stage fatigue cracks (sub-millimetre to a few millimetres) are undetectable** by visual survey — this is a fundamental limitation given that fatigue crack growth accelerates with length, so the visible-detection stage may already represent a meaningful fraction of remaining life before critical crack length.
- **NDT techniques** (dye penetrant, magnetic particle inspection) that can detect finer cracks are normally only applied at **specific suspect locations** identified by visual survey or by known fatigue-critical hot-spot lists (Q.6) — they are not, and cannot practically be, applied as blanket coverage across the entire structure, meaning cracks at unanticipated or newly-developing hot-spots (e.g., due to an unusual loading pattern or a fabrication defect not previously flagged) can go undetected until they become visually apparent or, in the worst case, until failure.
- Cracks hidden by **corrosion product, coating, or marine growth** can be physically obscured from visual detection even where a surveyor is looking directly at the location.

## 7. Human and Procedural Factors

- Survey quality is dependent on **surveyor experience, thoroughness, available time, and the completeness of staging/access provided by the owner/yard** — commercial pressure to minimize dry-dock duration can, in practice, compress the time available for thorough close-up survey and TM, particularly in less accessible areas.
- **Reporting thresholds and renewal criteria** (e.g., renewal at 75% of original thickness, or a stated percentage diminution) are themselves based on general structural strength/corrosion-margin assumptions (Q.3) and do not necessarily capture the **local structural consequence** of a given thickness loss at a stress-concentration point versus the same percentage loss on an uncritical flat plate area — a uniform numerical criterion cannot fully substitute for engineering judgment of local criticality.

## Overall Conclusion

Structural surveys and thickness measurement are **necessary but fundamentally probabilistic and incomplete tools** — they provide a statistically-sampled, point-in-time, partially-accessible picture of condition, filtered through the practical limits of available technology and time. They are most reliable for detecting and tracking **general, widespread wastage trends**, but are inherently less reliable for:
- **Localized pitting** (sampling density limitation),
- **Early-stage fatigue cracking** (visual detection threshold limitation),
- **Deterioration occurring between survey intervals** (time-snapshot limitation), and
- **Conditions hidden by coating or inaccessible geometry** (access/concealment limitation).

This is precisely why the industry has moved toward **risk-based, targeted survey planning** (identifying known hot-spots from casualty history, fatigue analysis, and coating condition, as discussed in Q.4 and Q.6) rather than relying solely on generic, uniform survey grids — recognizing that the limitations of the inspection method itself must be compensated for by directing the *available* inspection effort toward the locations statistically and mechanistically most likely to be critical, rather than assuming a passed survey represents a guarantee of structural soundness everywhere.
