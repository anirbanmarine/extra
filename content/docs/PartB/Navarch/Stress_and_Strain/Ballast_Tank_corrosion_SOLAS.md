## Ballast tank corrosion prevention as per SOLAS

SOLAS Chapter II-1, Regulation 3-2, together with the **IMO Performance Standard for Protective Coatings (PSPC) for Dedicated Seawater Ballast Tanks** (resolution MSC.215(82), adopted 2006, mandatory for ships contracted for construction on or after 1 July 2008), forms the regulatory basis for ballast tank corrosion prevention. It applies to all dedicated seawater ballast tanks of 150 gt and above and to the double-side skin spaces of bulk carriers 150 m and above.

## (a) Adherence to Performance Standards of Paint Coatings

**Design life target**
- PSPC sets a target coating life of **15 years**, defined as the period during which the coating system is expected to remain in "GOOD" condition (as per the IACS condition grading: GOOD = spot rusting on less than 3% of the area under consideration, with no visible failure/breakdown over the rest).
- This is not a guarantee but a **performance benchmark against which the coating system, application procedure, and surface preparation are all certified**.

**Key technical requirements under PSPC**
1. **Coating system selection**: Only coating systems that have passed a standardized **cyclic testing procedure** (as per IMO PSPC Annex 1) — which simulates 90 days of accelerated exposure combining seawater immersion, dry heat, condensation humidity, and UV exposure cycles, on both flat panels and specially designed edge/geometry test panels — are approved for use as "PSPC-compliant."
2. **Surface preparation standard**: Steel surfaces must be prepared to **Sa 2½ (near-white blast cleaning)** per ISO 8501-1, with a specified **surface profile (roughness) of 30–75 microns**, since coating adhesion and hence service life is highly sensitive to surface cleanliness and profile.
3. **Steel condition before coating**: Requirements on **primary surface preparation** (shop primer compatibility) and control of **soluble salt contamination** (chloride/salt contamination limits, typically ≤50 mg/m² NaCl equivalent) before coating, since residual salts under a coating film cause osmotic blistering and premature localized coating breakdown.
4. **Dry film thickness (DFT)**: A minimum **nominal total DFT of 320 microns** (two coats of stripe coating plus two full coats, or equivalent), with **90/10 rule compliance** — at least 90% of DFT measurements must meet or exceed nominal DFT, and no measurement may be below 90% of nominal DFT — ensuring the coating is neither under-applied (early breakdown) nor grossly uneven.
5. **Stripe coating requirement**: Edges, corners, welds, and other geometrically complex areas (exactly the transition zones discussed in Q.4) must receive an **additional stripe coat by brush or roller** before the main spray coats, because spray application alone cannot achieve adequate film thickness on sharp edges and corners — this is a direct regulatory response to the "difficulty of coating during service/construction" issue discussed below.
6. **Light-coloured topcoat**: A light colour finish is required specifically to make **inspection of coating breakdown, rust staining, and mechanical damage easier** during subsequent surveys.
7. **Coating Technical File (CTF)**: The shipyard must maintain a full record — surface preparation reports, environmental conditions during application (temperature, humidity, dew point), DFT measurement records, and inspection hold points — which becomes a permanent onboard/class record for verifying that PSPC compliance was actually achieved, not merely specified.

## (b) Importance of Initial Coating and the Difficulty of Coating During Service

**Why the initial (as-built) coating application is disproportionately important**

- Ballast tanks have highly **complex internal geometry** — stiffeners, brackets, manholes, air/sounding pipes, girders — and the *only* time in a ship's life when full, unrestricted access, proper ventilation, controlled temperature/humidity, and dedicated blasting/spraying equipment are simultaneously available is **during new construction at the shipyard**.
- Any deficiency in surface preparation or coating application at this stage — inadequate blast profile, insufficient stripe coating at brackets/toes, poor DFT control in way of complex framing — creates a **latent weak point that cannot be economically or technically corrected later**, and will manifest as early, localized coating breakdown once the tank enters seawater ballast service.
- This is precisely why PSPC places such heavy emphasis on **third-party verification of the initial coating process** (via a Coating Inspector qualified to NACE/FROSIO level, and mandatory hold-point inspections by the shipyard, coating manufacturer's representative, and often owner's representative) — the industry recognizes that **"getting it right the first time" is far more cost-effective and technically achievable than any subsequent in-service repair**, and that the vast majority of a coating's eventual service life is determined by the quality of this single initial application.

**Why coating repair/maintenance during service is difficult**

1. **Access constraints**: Ballast tanks in service can only be accessed when **empty and gas-freed**, which for a fully utilized vessel may mean limited windows during scheduled dry-docking or occasional deballasting for inspection — routine, unscheduled repair as soon as breakdown is noticed is often operationally impractical.
2. **Environmental control**: Shipboard tank atmospheres cannot replicate the shipyard's controlled temperature, humidity, and dew-point conditions; **coating applied in service is far more prone to condensation-induced adhesion failure and incomplete cure**, especially in tropical humidity or cold climates.
3. **Surface preparation limitations**: Full abrasive blasting to Sa 2½ inside an enclosed ballast tank on a loaded/operating ship is **extremely difficult, hazardous (confined space, dust, ventilation), and time-consuming** — in-service repairs frequently rely on hand/power tool cleaning (a lower standard, typically St 2/St 3), which achieves a lesser surface profile and consequently shorter repair-coating life.
4. **Confined space and safety hazards**: Ballast tank coating repair requires **confined space entry procedures, forced ventilation, and often hot work permits** near cargo tanks (fire/explosion risk considerations), all of which constrain the scope, timing, and thoroughness of repair work that can practically be undertaken.
5. **Cost and downtime**: Full re-coating of a ballast tank in service (as opposed to spot repair) is a **major, costly undertaking usually reserved for dry-docking**, meaning that once the original coating begins general breakdown (rather than isolated spot failure), the ship may operate for extended periods with a degraded coating system before a full recoat is economically undertaken — during which time corrosion (including the localized pitting mechanism discussed in Q.2) proceeds essentially unchecked in the breakdown areas.
6. **Progressive/cascading breakdown**: Once isolated coating failure occurs, the exposed bare steel corrodes and the resulting **rust scale further undermines the adhesion of adjacent intact coating** (edge creep/undercutting), meaning coating breakdown, once started, tends to spread progressively rather than remaining static — making early intervention important but, per the above points, operationally difficult to achieve promptly.

**Net regulatory logic**: Because in-service coating repair is inherently inferior in quality and difficult to execute compared to original shipyard application, PSPC's strategy is to **front-load quality assurance heavily at the newbuilding stage** (rigorous surface prep standards, stripe coating, DFT verification, documented Coating Technical File) so that the coating is far less likely to require major intervention within its 15-year design life, recognizing that the ballast tank environment will not readily permit an equivalent standard of repair once the ship is in service.
