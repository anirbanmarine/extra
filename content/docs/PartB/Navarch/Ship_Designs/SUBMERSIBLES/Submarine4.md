## Compare R.M. (righting moment) values of (a) surface monohull ship and (b) a submerged submarine with the help of diagrams showing positions of K, B, G and M.

## The Fundamental Difference in Stability Mechanism

Before comparing R.M. values, it is essential to understand that a **surface ship and a submerged submarine derive their transverse stability from entirely different geometric mechanisms**, even though both are ultimately governed by the same basic principle — the righting moment is the product of the **displacement (W) and the righting arm (GZ)**, i.e., **R.M. = W × GZ**. The difference lies in *how* GZ arises and behaves as the vessel heels, which fundamentally changes the shape and magnitude of the R.M. curve between the two cases.

## (a) Surface Monohull Ship — Stability Mechanism

**Positions of K, B, G, M:**
- **K** (keel, baseline reference) is at the bottom.
- **B** (centre of buoyancy) lies below the waterline, at the centroid of the underwater hull volume.
- **G** (centre of gravity) is typically **above B**, at a height determined by the vessel's loading and design.
- **M** (metacentre) is **above G** (for a stable ship) — the metacentre is the point about which the centre of buoyancy appears to pivot as the ship heels through small angles, and its height (KM) is a function of the underwater hull's **waterplane second moment of area (I)**: BM = I/∇ (where ∇ is the volume of displacement).

**Mechanism of righting moment generation:**
- As a surface ship heels, the **underwater hull shape changes** — one side emerges from the water while the other side becomes more deeply immersed, causing the **centre of buoyancy B to shift horizontally toward the low (immersed) side**.
- This horizontal shift of B, relative to the fixed position of G, creates the **righting arm GZ**, and hence the righting moment.
- Because this mechanism depends on the **change of underwater wedge shape (emerged wedge/immersed wedge) at the waterplane**, GM (metacentric height) and hence initial stability is strongly dependent on the ship's **waterplane area and beam** — a wide waterplane gives a large I, hence a large BM, hence typically a stable, positive GM.
- The R.M. curve (GZ curve) for a surface ship typically **rises from zero at zero heel, reaches a maximum GZ at a moderate angle of heel** (often 30–50° depending on hull form and freeboard), and then **declines, reaching zero (the angle of vanishing stability) at some larger angle** — commonly 60–90°+ for a well-found ship — beyond which the ship would capsize since GZ becomes negative.

## (b) Submerged Submarine — Stability Mechanism

**Positions of K, B, G, M:**
- For a **submerged submarine, the entire hull is completely enclosed by water** — there is no waterplane, no "emerged wedge/immersed wedge" effect possible, since the hull volume displaced does not change at all with heel angle (unlike a surface ship, whose displaced volume shape changes as it heels).
- Because of this, **B (centre of buoyancy) is fixed relative to the hull** — it does not shift as the submarine heels, since the underwater (fully submerged) volume and its shape relative to the hull remain constant regardless of heel angle.
- This is the single most important distinguishing point: **for a submerged submarine, M and B effectively coincide** (BM = 0, since there is no waterplane moment of inertia term to generate a metacentric radius) — i.e., **the metacentre M is at the same point as the centre of buoyancy B**.
- **G**, as with a surface ship, is positioned according to the vessel's mass distribution, and for a submarine, **G must be maintained below B** for the vessel to be transversely stable when submerged — this is why submarines are specifically designed and ballasted (via the tank arrangement in Q.2) to keep a **positive BG separation** (G below B) once dived.

**Mechanism of righting moment generation:**
- Since M coincides with B for the submerged case, the righting arm mechanism becomes simply the **direct "pendulum" effect of G being below B**: as the submarine heels, gravity acting through G and buoyancy acting through B (which does not move relative to the hull) create a righting couple analogous to a **weighted pendulum or a "keel-heavy" body** — the righting arm GZ = **BG × sin(heel angle)**, directly and simply, rather than through the more complex wedge-transfer mechanism of a surface ship.
- This is a **fundamentally more robust and "large-angle stable" mechanism** than the surface ship's waterplane-dependent stability: because the righting arm is generated by a fixed BG separation acting like a pendulum, **the submerged submarine's GZ curve is close to a pure sine curve, remaining positive through very large heel angles — theoretically even to 90° heel and, in principle, only vanishing at 180° (fully inverted)**, as long as G remains below B (i.e., BG stays positive) at all heel angles, which it inherently does for a submerged body since B does not move relative to the hull as it rotates.
- **Comparative R.M. magnitude**: For a given displacement, the actual **numerical value of BG for a submerged submarine is typically quite small** compared to the GM of a well-designed surface ship, since submarine designers deliberately keep G close to B (a large BG would require unnecessarily heavy keel ballast and waste buoyancy/payload capacity) — submarines are designed with just enough positive BG for adequate stability, not the generous GM margins used in surface ship design. So while the *range* of positive stability (angle of vanishing stability) for a submerged submarine is far greater than for a surface ship, the **magnitude of the righting moment at small angles may actually be comparable or even less** than a surface ship's, depending on the specific BG value chosen by the designer versus the ship's GM.

## Diagram Description (as would be drawn)

**Surface ship (heeled condition):**
- Waterline shown, hull cross-section heeled to one side.
- K at keel/baseline.
- B shown below the waterline, **displaced horizontally toward the low side** relative to the upright position (this horizontal shift is the key feature to illustrate).
- G shown on the vessel's centreline (fixed, does not move with heel — this is important to show as a contrast to B's movement).
- M shown above G on the vertical through the upright centre of buoyancy, essentially fixed for small angles (metacentre assumption valid up to ~10–15°).
- GZ shown as the horizontal distance between the vertical line through G and the vertical line through the (new) B — i.e., the perpendicular from G onto the line of action of the buoyancy force through B.

**Submarine (heeled condition, submerged):**
- Fully submerged hull cross-section, heeled to one side, with **no waterline/waterplane shown at all** (fully submerged — this is the key visual distinction from the surface ship diagram).
- B and M shown as **coincident, at the geometric centre of the submerged volume**, and this point does **not move relative to the hull** as the hull heels (contrast directly with the surface ship's B which does move).
- G shown **below B/M**, fixed relative to the hull.
- As the hull heels, the line KG (through the hull) rotates with the hull, but the vertical line of buoyancy action through B/M remains vertical (through the fixed point B=M) — the righting arm GZ is the horizontal separation that develops between G and the vertical through B as the hull rotates, forming the BG·sin(θ) relationship directly.

## Summary Table

| Parameter | Surface Monohull Ship | Submerged Submarine |
|---|---|---|
| **B movement with heel** | Shifts horizontally (wedge transfer) | Fixed relative to hull (no shift) |
| **M position** | Above G; BM = I/∇ (waterplane-dependent) | Coincides with B; BM ≈ 0 |
| **Stability-generating separation** | GM (metacentric height) | BG (centre of buoyancy above centre of gravity) |
| **GZ formula (small angle)** | GZ = GM × sin θ | GZ = BG × sin θ |
| **Range of positive stability** | Limited (typically ~60–90°) | Very large (theoretically to ~180°) |
| **Dependency** | Waterplane area/beam (I) | Fixed hull volume & mass distribution only |
