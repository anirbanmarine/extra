# Q.9 — Comparison of Mono-Hull with Multi-Hull Ship: Stability and Speed/Power Ratio Aspects

## Basic Configuration Difference

- A **monohull** ship has a single continuous hull form providing buoyancy, with stability derived from the waterplane area and beam of that single hull, as discussed in the surface-ship stability mechanism in Q.5.
- A **multi-hull** vessel (catamaran with two hulls, trimaran with three) distributes buoyancy across **two or more separated, generally slender hulls** connected by a bridging structure (cross-deck/wing deck), fundamentally changing both the stability mechanism and the resistance/powering characteristics compared to a monohull of equivalent displacement.

## Stability Comparison

**Monohull:**
- Transverse stability (GM) depends on **BM = I/∇**, where I is the waterplane's second moment of area about the centreline and ∇ is the displaced volume.
- For a monohull, achieving a large I relative to ∇ requires either a **wide beam relative to length/draft**, which increases resistance, or accepting a comparatively **modest GM**, particularly for slender, fine-lined hulls designed for speed.
- Roll motion in a seaway tends to have a **relatively long natural roll period** and a single, continuous roll pattern governed by the one hull's added mass and damping characteristics.

**Multi-hull:**
- The two (or more) hulls are set well apart (a wide **overall beam**), so the **effective "waterplane" for stability purposes is the combined moment of inertia of the two hulls acting at a large transverse separation** — the parallel-axis contribution (each hull's waterplane area multiplied by the square of its distance from the centreline) dominates the total I, producing a **very large I relative to the (comparatively small) total displaced volume** of the two slender hulls.
- This gives a multi-hull **exceptionally large GM and hence very high initial transverse stability** compared to a monohull of the same displacement — multihulls resist heeling strongly and have a **short, stiff roll period** (rapid, "snappy" roll motion) rather than the slower, easier roll of a monohull.
- **Trade-off**: this high initial stability comes with a **different failure mode** — because the righting arm mechanism depends on the geometric separation of the hulls, a multi-hull's stability, while very high at small-to-moderate heel angles, can **fall away rapidly beyond a certain critical heel angle** (e.g., when one hull begins to lift clear of the water or when the bridging structure starts to submerge/impact the sea), giving a comparatively **narrower range of positive stability at extreme angles** and a more sudden capsize characteristic once that critical angle is exceeded, compared to a well-formed monohull's more gradually-declining GZ curve.
- The **stiff, rapid roll motion** of multihulls, while safe from a stability-magnitude standpoint, can produce **higher accelerations and reduced comfort/seakeeping** for personnel and equipment compared to the gentler, longer-period roll of a monohull — an important seakeeping trade-off alongside the pure stability comparison.

## Speed / Power Ratio Comparison

**Monohull:**
- A single hull of a given displacement must be proportionately **beamier and fuller** to carry that displacement, resulting in **higher wave-making resistance at high Froude numbers** (speed-to-length ratio) due to the fuller underwater form.
- Resistance rises steeply as speed increases toward and beyond the hull speed (where wavemaking resistance becomes dominant), requiring a **disproportionately large increase in installed power for further speed gains** — the classic monohull "power wall" at high Froude number.

**Multi-hull:**
- Each individual hull of a catamaran/trimaran can be made **very slender (high length-to-beam ratio)** for the same total displacement, since displacement is now shared between two (or more) hulls rather than carried by one — a slender hull form has **substantially lower wave-making resistance** for a given speed than a single beamy hull carrying the same total displacement.
- This allows a multi-hull vessel to achieve a **significantly better speed-to-power ratio** — i.e., higher speed for the same installed power, or equivalently, less power required for the same speed — compared to an equivalent-displacement monohull, particularly pronounced at higher Froude numbers/speed-length ratios, which is precisely why multi-hull configurations are favoured for high-speed craft (fast ferries, naval fast attack craft) rather than for slow, heavy-displacement cargo carriers where the benefit is less significant relative to the added structural/cost complexity.
- **Trade-off**: the total **wetted surface area of two (or more) slender hulls is greater** than that of a single hull of equivalent displacement, which increases **frictional resistance** — at **low speed**, where frictional resistance dominates total resistance (wave-making being comparatively small at low Froude number), a multi-hull can actually be **less efficient (higher resistance) than a monohull**, meaning the multi-hull's powering advantage is speed-regime dependent, being most pronounced at higher speeds and comparatively disadvantageous at low speeds/displacement-mode operation.
- Additional powering/structural considerations: the **bridging structure connecting the hulls** must be strong enough to resist the differential loading between the two hulls in a seaway (each hull can be lifted/depressed independently by wave action), adding structural weight that partially offsets the pure hull-resistance advantage, and **wave interference effects between the two hulls' wave systems** can either favourably or unfavourably affect total resistance depending on hull spacing and speed, requiring careful hull-spacing design (a consideration entirely absent in monohull design).

## Summary Table

| Aspect | Monohull | Multi-hull (Catamaran/Trimaran) |
|---|---|---|
| **Initial GM/stability** | Moderate (beam-limited) | Very high (wide effective waterplane via hull separation) |
| **Roll period** | Longer, gentler roll | Short, stiff, rapid roll |
| **Range of positive stability** | Gradual decline, larger range at extreme angles | High initial stability but can fall away sharply beyond critical heel |
| **Wave-making resistance at high speed** | High (fuller hull form) | Low (slender individual hulls) |
| **Frictional resistance** | Lower (less wetted surface) | Higher (two/three hulls' wetted surface) |
| **Speed/power ratio at high speed** | Comparatively poorer | Significantly better |
| **Speed/power ratio at low speed** | Comparatively better | Can be poorer (friction-dominated) |
| **Structural complexity** | Simpler, single continuous hull | Added bridging structure, differential loading between hulls |
| **Best application** | General cargo/displacement-speed vessels | High-speed craft, ferries, fast naval vessels |
