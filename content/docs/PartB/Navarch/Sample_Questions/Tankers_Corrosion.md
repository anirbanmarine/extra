Corrosion in tankers is dangerous not only because it thins steel, but because it removes strength from highly stressed, poorly accessible parts of the hull girder—often at boundaries between warm oil tanks and cold seawater ballast spaces. Modern SOLAS coating standards and enhanced survey programmes reduce the risk, but inspection must combine close-up visual examination, coating assessment, ultrasonic thickness measurement, structural judgement and timely repair; no single method is sufficient. [ww2.eagle](https://ww2.eagle.org/content/dam/eagle/rules-and-guides/current/survey_and_inspection/49_application_inspection_marine_coating_systems_2017/Coatings_GN_e-Jan17.pdf)

## Q1. VLCC vapour-space corrosion

The **vapour space** is the volume in a cargo tank above the liquid cargo level. In a VLCC carrying crude oil, it is commonly one of the most aggressive corrosion environments in the ship.

```text
          Cargo-tank deck plating
     ─────────────────────────────────
       Vapour space: gases + moisture
       ↑ corrosive condensate zone
     ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~  Cargo oil surface
       Liquid crude-oil cargo
```

### Reasons for accelerated corrosion

1. **Acidic gases occur in the inert-gas atmosphere**

   Cargo tanks are commonly inerted to reduce explosion risk. However, inert gas and crude-oil vapours may contain oxygen, carbon dioxide, sulphur dioxide and hydrogen sulphide. In the presence of condensed moisture, these gases form corrosive aqueous films and acidic products on deck plating, underdeck longitudinals and upper side structure. Field examinations of VLCC cargo tanks have found notable quantities of \(O_2\), \(CO_2\), \(SO_2\) and \(H_2S\), including high \(H_2S\) concentrations in vapour spaces. [classnk.or](https://www.classnk.or.jp/hp/pdf/publications/Publications_image/classnk_magazine_no63.pdf)

   A simplified corrosion sequence is:

   \[
   SO_2 + H_2O \rightarrow \text{acidic sulphur compounds}
   \]

   \[
   CO_2 + H_2O \rightarrow H_2CO_3
   \]

   These wet, acidic films accelerate oxidation of steel:

   \[
   Fe \rightarrow Fe^{2+}+2e^-
   \]

2. **Condensation and wet–dry cycling**

   The upper tank structure is alternately heated by cargo and cooled by night air, seawater or ballast water in adjacent spaces. Water vapour condenses on the relatively cool steel, producing an electrolyte film. Repeated wetting and drying concentrates salts and acidic contaminants, often making corrosion more severe than continuous immersion corrosion.

3. **High cargo temperature**

   Some crude oils are loaded warm, and heated cargoes create significant temperature gradients. The vapour space, deck and upper shell are subjected to thermal cycling. Higher temperature generally increases electrochemical reaction rates and promotes condensation on adjacent cooler surfaces.

4. **Oxygen is not always absent**

   Inerting reduces oxygen concentration but does not make every local environment chemically inert. Tank opening, loading/discharging, tank washing, imperfect inert gas quality, leaks and local air ingress can introduce oxygen. Corrosion is particularly severe where water, oxygen and corrosive gases coexist.

5. **Salt contamination**

   Seawater contamination, salt deposits, ballast-tank leakage, washing water, poor tank cleaning and humid marine atmosphere can create chloride-rich electrolyte films. Chlorides promote local attack and interfere with coating durability.

6. **Coating damage and poor access**

   The underside of deck plating, deck longitudinals, bracket toes, welds and upper-frame connections are difficult to blast, coat, inspect and repair. Damage to protective coating exposes bare steel, and local corrosion cells form rapidly at edges, welds and damaged areas.

### Typical locations of severe vapour-space corrosion

```text
        Freeboard deck / upper deck
  ─────────────────────────────────────
  Underdeck longitudinals  ← major target
  Brackets and weld toes   ← local attack
  Upper transverse webs    ← coating damage
  Upper wing-tank boundary ← thermal gradient
  ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~  oil surface
```

The most important inspection targets are:

- Underside of deck plating.
- Deck longitudinals and their web/flange edges.
- Brackets, scallops, weld toes and cut-outs.
- Upper parts of transverse bulkheads and longitudinal bulkheads.
- Upper side-shell and upper wing-tank interfaces.
- Heating-coil supports and fittings, where fitted.

## Q2. Pitting corrosion and pollution risk

### Definition

**Pitting corrosion** is highly localized corrosion that produces small, deep cavities or pits in steel. It differs from general corrosion, which thins a broad area more uniformly.

```text
General wastage                    Pitting corrosion

Steel surface                      Steel surface
──────────────                     ────┬──────┬────
  ↓ uniform thinning                   │  ↓   │
──────────────                     ────┴─┐ ┌─┴────
                                            └── deep pit
```

A plate may appear generally acceptable, yet a few pits can penetrate a large fraction of its original thickness.

### Why pitting occurs in cargo tanks

Pitting is caused by local electrochemical cells created where conditions differ over small areas of steel.

Typical causes are:

- Breakdown, blistering, cracking or mechanical damage to coating.
- Water droplets, tank-wash water or salt deposits trapped in local depressions.
- Chloride contamination from seawater.
- Oxygen concentration differences between the centre and edges of a wet deposit.
- Acidic condensate in the vapour space.
- Sludge, scale, cargo residues or microbial activity that create differential aeration cells.
- Weld defects, rough surfaces, edges, attachment points and coating holidays.
- Galvanic differences between bare steel, coated steel, weld metal and deposits.

### Pollution consequences

Pitting is particularly serious in:

- Bottom plating of cargo tanks.
- Inner-bottom plating in double-hull tankers.
- Bulkheads separating cargo and ballast tanks.
- Cargo tank boundaries adjacent to void spaces.
- Shell plating in single-hull tankers.

```text
Cargo oil tank                    Sea / ballast space

  Oil cargo
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
        steel plate
───────────────┐
      pitting  │  deepens
        ↓      │
───────────┐   │
           └───┘  → perforation → oil leakage
```

The pollution potential rises because pitting can produce a **through-thickness perforation without a dramatic visible structural failure**. Oil can leak:

- Into a ballast tank or void space, becoming difficult to detect immediately.
- Into the double bottom, contaminating ballast or bilge systems.
- Directly to sea in a single-hull or externally damaged boundary.
- Into spaces where vapour accumulation also creates fire and explosion hazards.

A small pit can also act as a crack initiator. If it occurs in a highly stressed plate, cyclic loading may turn local wastage into a fatigue crack and then into a larger rupture.

## Q3. Why deck and inner-bottom scantlings reduced

This question usually refers to design trends in earlier generations of tankers, especially where designers took advantage of improved structural arrangements and higher-strength steels. It should not be understood to mean that present rules permit unsafe steel reduction: current Common Structural Rules prescribe net scantlings, corrosion additions and minimum thicknesses. For example, modern rules impose specific inner-bottom requirements and set gross minimum thickness values for structural members in liquid tanks and double bottoms. [iacs.s3.af-south-1.amazonaws](https://iacs.s3.af-south-1.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/14140654/csr_double_hull_oil_tanker_pdf778.pdf)

### Main reasons

| Reason | Effect on scantling selection |
|---|---|
| **Use of higher-tensile steel** | Higher yield strength permits a smaller required plate thickness for the same calculated strength, subject to buckling, fatigue and minimum-thickness requirements |
| **Double-hull structural action** | Inner bottom and inner side participate in hull-girder strength; load paths can be distributed through more longitudinal material |
| **Finite-element analysis** | More accurate modelling may show that older empirical rules were conservative in certain locations, permitting optimized local scantlings |
| **Improved longitudinal framing** | Better continuous longitudinals, web frames, girders and bracket design reduce local bending demand and allow material to be placed where it is structurally most effective |
| **Weight and deadweight economics** | Steel saved from deck, bottom or internal structure reduces lightship weight and can increase payload, improve fuel efficiency or reduce construction cost |
| **Construction methods and material efficiency** | Automated welding, improved steel quality, standardized panels and optimized structure encouraged “minimum-weight” design practice |
| **Reliance on corrosion additions and survey controls** | Design may be based on a required net thickness plus a specified corrosion margin, assuming coatings, maintenance and surveys will preserve the intended net structural thickness |

### The safety concern

The drawback of reduced initial scantlings is a lower margin for:

- Unexpected corrosion.
- Coating failure.
- Local pitting.
- Fatigue cracking.
- Damage during repair or cargo operations.
- Incomplete survey detection.
- Higher-than-assumed wave loads and sloshing loads.

This is why modern structural rules distinguish between:

\[
t_{\text{gross}}
=
t_{\text{net}}
+
t_{\text{corrosion addition}}
\]

If corrosion consumes the allowance faster than expected, the steel may reach its net structural limit long before a casual visual inspection suggests serious danger.

## Q4. Casualties and high-risk transition zones

### Why ballast-tank boundaries are vulnerable

Ballast tanks are a severe corrosion environment because they contain seawater, oxygen, chlorides, temperature variation and frequent wet–dry cycles. The most severe areas are often those adjacent to cargo tanks.

```text
Warm oil cargo tank       Common bulkhead        Cold seawater ballast tank

   Heated cargo        │ steel boundary │          seawater + oxygen
~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~│────────────────│~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
                       │                │
    Thermal cycling →  │ condensation → │ coating breakdown/corrosion
```

A bulkhead or plating panel can suffer corrosion from both sides:

- Cargo-tank side: acidic vapour condensate, cargo residues and temperature effects.
- Ballast-tank side: seawater corrosion, oxygen, chlorides, coating breakdown and microbial effects.

This double-sided deterioration reduces section modulus more rapidly than corrosion on one face alone.

### Why transition zones are critical

A **transition zone** is where structural geometry, stiffness, material thickness, loading type or environment changes abruptly. Such zones concentrate stress and are preferred sites for fatigue initiation.

Examples include:

- Boundary between cargo tank and ballast tank.
- Connection of longitudinal bulkhead to transverse bulkhead.
- Tank-top to hopper plating transition.
- Deck longitudinal termination.
- Bracket toes and end connections.
- Cut-outs, lightening holes and scallops.
- Change from high-tensile to mild steel.
- Change in plate thickness.
- End of a longitudinal girder.
- Pump-room boundaries.
- Sloping bulkheads and knuckles.

```text
Longitudinal stiffener termination

───────────────┐
               │  abrupt stiffness change
               └───────
                  ↑
            fatigue/crack-prone zone
```

### Tanker casualties

**Erika, 1999.** The single-hull tanker broke apart in heavy weather off France, causing major oil pollution. The case is widely associated with structural deterioration, corrosion and the vulnerability of an ageing single-hull tanker. It helped intensify regulatory pressure for accelerated phase-out of single-hull oil tankers.

**Prestige, 2002.** The Prestige suffered a hull breach and subsequently broke up off Spain, causing a major spill. The Bahamas investigation identified a probable initial breach associated with a large wave exposing weakness in No. 3 starboard wing tank; it specifically noted that corrosion-related weakening of tanker ballast-tank structure is a well-documented problem and that the reported breaches were consistent with such weakness. [bahamasmaritime](https://www.bahamasmaritime.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/BMA-Investigation-Report-Hull-failure-and-loss-of-the-Prestige.pdf)

**Nakhodka, 1997.** The tanker broke in severe weather off Japan, leading to heavy fuel-oil pollution. The incident is often used to illustrate the vulnerability of older single-hull tanker structures to combined wave loading, ageing and deterioration.

**Aegean Sea, 1992.** Grounding and hull damage off La Coruña led to oil release and fire; while this is principally a grounding case, it demonstrates why loss of shell integrity in a loaded oil tanker has major environmental consequences.

### Examination comment

A strong conclusion is:

> Corrosion does not act in isolation. It reduces thickness, which increases stress; increased stress promotes fatigue; fatigue cracks concentrate at details and transition zones; and a large wave, grounding event or sloshing load may then trigger a sudden structural failure. The danger is greatest where a common boundary plate is exposed to corrosive environments on both sides and where geometry creates stress concentration.

## Q5. Ballast-tank corrosion prevention

### SOLAS protective-coating requirements

SOLAS Chapter II-1 includes mandatory protective-coating requirements through the **Performance Standard for Protective Coatings (PSPC)**.

- **SOLAS II-1/3-2** applies the PSPC to dedicated seawater ballast tanks in all ship types and to double-side skin spaces of bulk carriers.
- **SOLAS II-1/3-11** applies PSPC requirements to cargo oil tanks of crude-oil tankers.
- The prescribed coating systems are intended to achieve a target useful coating life of **15 years**. [ww2.eagle](https://ww2.eagle.org/content/dam/eagle/rules-and-guides/current/survey_and_inspection/49_application_inspection_marine_coating_systems_2017/Coatings_GN_e-Jan17.pdf)

For tanker ballast tanks, the relevant concept is PSPC for dedicated seawater ballast tanks, commonly linked to IMO Resolution MSC.215(82) and subsequent amendments. [iims-media-library.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws](https://iims-media-library.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/22144746/Performance-standards-for-corrosion-protection-2024.pdf)

### What a compliant prevention strategy requires

```text
Good design
     +
Correct surface preparation
     +
Specified coating system
     +
Controlled application
     +
Documented inspection
     +
Maintenance and repairs
     =
Longer corrosion-free service life
```

### 1. Compliance with coating performance standards

Key elements include:

- Steel surface preparation to the specified cleanliness grade.
- Removal of weld spatter, sharp edges and surface imperfections.
- Correct stripe coating at edges, welds, brackets, scallops and difficult details.
- Correct dry-film thickness.
- Controlled temperature, dew point, relative humidity and steel-surface condition during application.
- Inspection and documentation during construction.
- Coating technical file and maintenance records.
- Use of a qualified coating inspector and appropriate owner/class/shipyard quality control.

The coating is a **system**, not merely a layer of paint. Poor edge preparation, inadequate stripe coating or application outside environmental limits can cause early failure even if the nominal dry-film thickness appears satisfactory.

### 2. Why the initial coating is crucial

The first coating application is the most important because it is applied in a controlled newbuilding environment before the tank enters service.

A high-quality initial coating:

- Prevents corrosion before it begins.
- Reduces under-film corrosion.
- Keeps the tank surface smooth and easier to inspect.
- Reduces later steel renewal and repair cost.
- Limits the formation of pits that may become crack initiators.
- Makes subsequent maintenance more practical.

Poor initial coating can lead to:

```text
Minor holiday / edge defect
        ↓
Under-film corrosion
        ↓
Blistering and coating disbondment
        ↓
Large exposed area
        ↓
General wastage + pitting
        ↓
Steel renewal / structural risk
```

### 3. Why in-service recoating is difficult

Repairing coating in a ballast tank is much more difficult than applying the original coating because:

- Access is restricted and ventilation is limited.
- The space is enclosed, creating health and gas-safety hazards.
- Surfaces are contaminated with salt, rust, oil, scale and old coating residues.
- Complete abrasive blasting is difficult around complex stiffeners, brackets and pipework.
- Humidity, temperature and dew point may not remain within ideal application limits.
- Tank schedules compete with cargo operations, dry-docking and commercial pressure.
- Temporary ventilation, lighting, staging and safety arrangements are costly.
- Spot repair may leave adjacent marginal coating that subsequently fails.

Hence the practical rule is:

> It is far cheaper and safer to achieve high-quality surface preparation and coating at newbuilding than to recover a severely corroded ballast tank after years of service.

### Survey consequences of poor coating

Where hard protective coating was not applied at construction, is soft or semi-hard, is in poor condition and not repaired, or substantial corrosion is found, ballast tanks are subject to annual examination requirements in the enhanced survey regime. [classnk.or](https://www.classnk.or.jp/hp/pdf/info_service/iacs_ur_and_ui/ur_z10.4_rev.17_may_2022ul.pdf)

## Q6. Fatigue cracks: causes and inspection targets

### Why fatigue cracks occur

Fatigue cracking occurs when a structural detail experiences repeated cyclic stress. The stress range may be below the steel’s static yield strength, but repeated thousands or millions of times it can initiate and propagate a crack.

```text
Repeated wave loading

Hogging → sagging → hogging → sagging → hogging
   ↑          ↓
Repeated stress range at structural detail
                     ↓
               fatigue crack
```

The principal sources of cyclic loading are:

- Hull-girder hogging and sagging in waves.
- Torsional loading, especially near large deck openings or discontinuities.
- Sloshing loads in partly filled tanks.
- Pressure cycling in ballast tanks.
- Vibration from main engines, shafting, propellers and pumps.
- Thermal expansion and contraction.
- Repeated local bending of stiffeners and brackets.
- Cargo loading/discharging cycles.
- Berthing, grounding and impact loads.

### Where to look

Routine inspection should focus on places with high stress concentration, reduced stiffness or known coating damage:

- Weld toes and weld ends.
- Bracket toes and bracket terminations.
- Stiffener end connections.
- Connections between longitudinals and transverse webs.
- Cut-outs, manholes, lightening holes and scallops.
- Corners of openings.
- Change in plate thickness.
- Change in framing system.
- Intersections of bulkheads, girders and stringers.
- Deck longitudinals near hatch ends and deckhouse ends.
- Tank-top and inner-bottom connections.
- Transverse web frame connections in wing ballast tanks.
- Pump-room bulkheads and lower stool areas.
- Bilge wells, drain holes and areas below sounding pipes.
- Areas around cargo heating systems and pipe supports.

```text
Web-frame detail

        Longitudinal
────────────────────────
          │
          │ weld toe  ← inspect
          │
     ┌────┴────┐
     │ bracket │ ← inspect bracket toe
─────┴─────────┴───── plating
       ↑
    scallop / cut-out
    high stress region
```

### Areas needing stringent inspection

More stringent close-up survey and thickness gauging should target:

- Cargo-tank deck structure in the vapour space.
- Upper parts of transverse bulkheads.
- Ballast-tank transverse webs and web-frame connections.
- Lower parts of wing tanks and hopper-like regions where water and sediment collect.
- Inner-bottom plating adjacent to cargo-tank/ballast-tank boundaries.
- Shell plating and internal structure in the midship \(0.4L\) region, where hull-girder bending is highest.
- Known substantial-corrosion areas and adjacent structure.
- Previously repaired cracks, doublers, insert plates and weld repairs.
- Locations with poor, rusted, blistered or detached coating.

Modern survey guidance requires thickness measurement to represent both general and local corrosion. Where substantial corrosion is identified, measurements must be expanded before the survey can be completed. [iacs.s3.af-south-1.amazonaws](https://iacs.s3.af-south-1.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/20152627/rec84rev1.pdf)

## Q7. Limitations of surveys and thickness measurements

Thickness measurement—normally ultrasonic—is essential, but it cannot by itself establish whether a tanker is structurally safe.

### Limitation 1: Measurements are discrete samples

Ultrasonic readings are taken at selected points, whereas corrosion may be highly localized.

```text
Measured points:       Hidden pit between points:

•       •       •      •       •       •
──────────────────     ────────▼────────
                           deep pit missed
```

A few readings may show acceptable average thickness while a small pit or groove between points is near perforation.

### Limitation 2: General wastage versus local damage

UT is good for measuring remaining thickness at the measurement point, but interpretation is required to distinguish:

- Uniform general corrosion.
- Local pitting.
- Grooving along welds.
- Edge corrosion.
- Crack-like defects.
- Lamination or internal defects.
- Under-deposit corrosion.

A plate may satisfy average-thickness criteria while still having unacceptable local pitting or severe grooving.

### Limitation 3: Surface condition affects reliability

Rust scale, coating, roughness, marine deposits and poor probe contact can affect reading quality. Calibration, competent operators and surveyor oversight are therefore necessary. IACS guidance states that ultrasonic equipment accuracy must be proven and that measurements should be representative of the nature and extent of wastage; qualified companies perform the work and surveyors verify it. [iacs.s3.af-south-1.amazonaws](https://iacs.s3.af-south-1.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/20152627/rec84rev1.pdf)

### Limitation 4: Access restrictions

Many critical locations are difficult to inspect:

- High underdeck areas.
- Behind piping and cables.
- Inside narrow wing tanks.
- Bracket toes and small scallops.
- Areas behind stiffeners.
- Tank corners and bilges.
- Structures obscured by scale or coating.
- Deep tanks requiring staging, rafting or remote inspection tools.

If access is poor, a survey can become biased toward accessible, less critical surfaces.

### Limitation 5: Coating can conceal corrosion

A coating may look intact while corrosion progresses beneath it. Conversely, a visibly poor coating does not always indicate the exact remaining steel thickness. Coating condition is a warning indicator, not a substitute for thickness gauging.

### Limitation 6: UT does not reliably detect cracks

Normal thickness gauging is not a crack-detection technique. Fatigue cracks need close visual inspection and, when required:

- Magnetic particle inspection.
- Dye penetrant testing.
- Ultrasonic flaw detection.
- Eddy-current testing.
- Appropriate non-destructive testing of weld details.

### Limitation 7: Structure is a system, not a set of plates

The residual strength of a tanker depends on more than individual plate thickness:

\[
\text{Residual structural capacity}
=
f(\text{plate thickness},\text{stiffeners},\text{buckling},\text{fatigue},\text{connections},\text{load path},\text{global bending})
\]

A structure may fail by local buckling, stiffener tripping, bracket failure, crack propagation or loss of continuity before a simple plate-thickness limit is reached.

### Limitation 8: Corrosion can progress between surveys

Survey intervals create a time gap. In severe ballast-tank or cargo-vapour-space environments, coating failure and corrosion may develop quickly between scheduled examinations, especially if the vessel experiences unusual heating, poor ballast-water quality, extended idle periods or damage.

### Limitation 9: Human and commercial factors

Survey effectiveness may be reduced by:

- Incomplete tank cleaning.
- Inadequate lighting or staging.
- Time pressure in dry dock.
- Incomplete access.
- Inadequate documentation of prior repairs.
- Poor communication between owner, class, yard and thickness-measurement company.
- Over-reliance on minimum numerical compliance rather than engineering judgement.

## Exam-ready conclusion

- VLCC cargo-tank vapour spaces corrode rapidly because warm cargo, condensed moisture, oxygen, sulphur-bearing gases and difficult-to-maintain coatings create an aggressive acidic wet–dry environment. [classnk.or](https://www.classnk.or.jp/hp/pdf/publications/Publications_image/classnk_magazine_no63.pdf)
- Pitting is localized deep attack; it can perforate a tank boundary, initiate fatigue cracking and create direct oil-pollution risk.
- Reduced scantlings improve economy and deadweight but reduce tolerance to unexpected corrosion and fatigue; this is why corrosion margins, minimum thicknesses, coating standards and enhanced surveys are essential. [iacs.s3.af-south-1.amazonaws](https://iacs.s3.af-south-1.amazonaws.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/14140654/csr_double_hull_oil_tanker_pdf778.pdf)
- The highest-risk locations are common boundaries between cargo and ballast tanks and structural discontinuities such as bracket toes, welds, cut-outs and changes in stiffness.
- SOLAS PSPC requirements aim for a 15-year coating target life and place major importance on initial surface preparation, correct application and documentation. [ww2.eagle](https://ww2.eagle.org/content/dam/eagle/rules-and-guides/current/survey_and_inspection/49_application_inspection_marine_coating_systems_2017/Coatings_GN_e-Jan17.pdf)
- Ultrasonic gauging is necessary but cannot replace close-up inspection, crack detection, structural assessment and proactive coating maintenance.
