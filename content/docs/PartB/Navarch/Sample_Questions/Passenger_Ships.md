IMO’s passenger-ship safety initiative is built on the idea that prevention comes first, but if a casualty occurs, the ship should remain survivable—ideally acting as its own “best lifeboat” while returning safely to port or supporting an orderly abandonment. The initiative became necessary because very large cruise ships can carry several thousand people, have complex “hotel” spaces and fire loads, and can overwhelm normal evacuation and search-and-rescue arrangements even when formally compliant with older SOLAS and Load Line provisions. [imo](https://www.imo.org/en/ourwork/safety/pages/passengerships.aspx)

## Five pillars

| Pillar | Meaning | Practical example |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Prevention** | Reduce the probability of fire, collision, grounding, flooding, and operational error before they become casualties | Improved fire prevention in atria and accommodation, improved escape arrangements, bridge/navigation safety, contingency planning, and passenger-ship-specific STCW training in crowd management and crisis management.  [imo](https://www.imo.org/en/ourwork/safety/pages/passengerships.aspx) |
| **2. Improved survivability** | Design the ship so that, after defined damage, people can remain safe on board and the ship can proceed under its own power to a port of refuge | Redundant propulsion, steering, electrical distribution, fire main, communications, detection, bilge/ballast and watertight-door systems; safe areas; emergency management from a safety centre.  [imo](https://www.imo.org/en/ourwork/safety/pages/passengerships.aspx) |
| **3. Regulatory flexibility** | Permit innovative technologies and arrangements without reducing the required safety outcome | SOLAS alternative design and arrangements provisions permit a designer to demonstrate, through rigorous assessment, that a novel fire-safety or evacuation arrangement achieves at least an equivalent level of safety.  [imo](https://www.imo.org/en/ourwork/safety/pages/summaryofsolaschapterii-2-default.aspx) |
| **4. Operation remote from SAR facilities** | Recognize that rapid outside rescue may be impossible in polar, oceanic, remote-island, or poorly served areas | Voyage planning, external-support arrangements with SAR authorities, recovery techniques, and procedures for rescuing people from survival craft or the water.  [imo](https://www.imo.org/en/ourwork/safety/pages/passengerships.aspx) |
| **5. Health, safety and medical care** | Maintain passenger survival and wellbeing during an extended onboard emergency or evacuation | Medical-safety programmes, safe-area medical capacity, sanitation, water, food, ventilation, temperature control and cold-water survival guidance.  [imo](https://www.imo.org/en/ourwork/safety/pages/passengerships.aspx) |

A useful exam conclusion is: the five pillars move safety from a narrow **“abandon ship quickly”** philosophy toward **“prevent, contain, survive, return safely, and evacuate only when necessary.”**

## Why new standards were needed

Large cruise ships were already expected to comply with applicable SOLAS and ILLC requirements. IMO’s concern was whether regulations, many drafted before the newest very large passenger ships existed, fully addressed the operational reality of an emergency on such ships. [imo](https://www.imo.org/en/ourwork/safety/pages/passengerships.aspx)

### Scale of persons on board

A large cruise ship may have thousands of passengers plus crew. The consequences of a single major fire, blackout, collision, grounding, or flooding event can therefore be disproportionate.

Evacuating such a population is difficult because many passengers:

- Are unfamiliar with ship layout, alarm signals, muster procedures and lifeboat embarkation.
- May be elderly, disabled, injured, accompanied by children, or unable to communicate in the working language of the crew.
- May panic, move against crew instructions, become separated from families, or congest stairways and embarkation areas.

The key question was not simply whether the ship carried enough lifeboat seats. It was whether people could **reach**, board, launch, survive in, and be recovered from survival craft in severe weather.

### Fire vulnerability

Cruise ships contain high fire loads and complex hotel-type spaces:

- Cabins, restaurants, galleys, laundries, theatres, retail areas and entertainment spaces.
- Long accommodation corridors and multi-deck vertical spaces such as atria.
- Extensive HVAC systems that can spread smoke if not correctly isolated.
- Electrical and machinery systems whose loss may impair detection, communication, propulsion, lighting, pumps and evacuation.

IMO specifically recognized fire as a particular vulnerability of large cruise ships because hotel services create inherent ignition and fire-spread risks. [imo](https://www.imo.org/en/ourwork/safety/pages/passengerships.aspx)

### Survivability rather than immediate abandonment

Traditional regulations were strongly oriented to lifesaving appliances and evacuation. The large-passenger-ship initiative recognized that, in difficult weather or remote waters, abandoning a large ship may be more dangerous than remaining aboard a damaged but stable, powered and habitable vessel.

Thus, for casualties below a defined threshold, the design target is:

```text
Casualty occurs
      ↓
Detect and contain it
      ↓
Keep propulsion, steering, power and communications available
      ↓
Move passengers into protected safe areas
      ↓
Return safely to port under own propulsion
```

If the casualty exceeds the threshold, the ship must still remain viable long enough for controlled evacuation and abandonment. IMO defined the “time for orderly evacuation and abandonment” as the time from the casualty threshold being exceeded until all persons have abandoned safely; the design work considered a viability period of at least three hours. [imo](https://www.imo.org/en/ourwork/safety/pages/passengerships.aspx)

## Training requirements

### Existing STCW requirements

Under STCW Regulation V/2 and Code section A‑V/2, training is assigned according to a person’s duties.

| Personnel | Required competence |
|---|---|
| Crew or other personnel listed to assist passengers during an emergency | **Crowd management** training |
| Masters, chief mates, chief engineers, second engineers and persons responsible for passenger safety in emergency situations | Approved **crisis management and human behaviour** training |
| Personnel with safety duties | Familiarization with emergency procedures, use of lifesaving appliances, emergency communications, passenger assistance and shipboard muster roles |
| All seafarers | Basic safety training, including firefighting, personal survival, first aid and personal safety/social responsibility, as applicable |

Crowd-management competence covers controlling passengers during emergencies, locating and assisting people needing help, communicating clearly, managing passenger movement and avoiding panic. Crisis-management and human-behaviour competence includes organizing emergency procedures, using available resources, maintaining communications and controlling the human response to an emergency. [wwwcdn.imo](https://wwwcdn.imo.org/localresources/en/OurWork/Safety/Documents/RORO.pdf)

### Why this may still be inadequate

The weakness is often not the existence of certificates, but the gap between classroom competence and a complex real casualty involving multiple failures, smoke, darkness, language barriers, injury, loss of communications and rapidly changing stability.

A serious casualty can combine:

```text
Fire or flooding
+ loss of electrical power
+ smoke / impaired visibility
+ disabled lifts and communications
+ frightened passengers
+ partial loss of propulsion or steering
+ difficult weather
= a systems-and-human-factors emergency
```

Training can be inadequate where drills are too predictable, announced in advance, conducted in benign weather, or restricted to individual departments rather than testing bridge–engine–hotel–security–medical coordination.

### Examples of shortcomings

**Herald of Free Enterprise (1987).** The ferry sailed with bow doors open. The immediate technical condition was simple—water entered the vehicle deck—but the underlying failures included weak operational controls, unclear responsibility, inadequate verification of door closure, and poor safety management. Water on the undivided vehicle deck rapidly caused a severe free-surface effect and capsize. This illustrates why bridge teams, vehicle-deck personnel and senior officers need robust closed-loop reporting and challenge-response procedures, not merely familiarity with a checklist.

**Scandinavian Star (1990).** The fire demonstrated the importance of competent firefighting command, smoke control, communications, emergency lighting, passenger guidance and crew familiarity with the vessel. A passenger vessel’s crew must be able to keep escape routes tenable and direct people away from smoke, not merely fight the fire.

**Estonia (1994).** The loss highlighted the fatal consequences of rapid ingress onto a ro-ro deck. The event requires effective damage recognition, watertight-integrity management, immediate reduction of speed or course alteration where appropriate, passenger control and timely—but orderly—evacuation decisions.

### Additional training proposed

A credible enhanced programme should include the following:

- **Integrated casualty drills:** bridge, engine room, safety centre, firefighting teams, hotel department, medical team, security and evacuation teams exercise together.
- **Scenario-based fire drills:** fire in an atrium, galley, cabin corridor, laundry, vehicle deck or machinery space; include smoke migration, ventilation shutdown and a blocked escape route.
- **Flooding and damage-control drills:** simulated progressive flooding, watertight-door management, bilge/pump limitations, stability assessment and safe-area relocation.
- **Blackout drills:** loss of main power, degraded communications, emergency generator operation, emergency steering, emergency lighting and manual procedures.
- **Safe-return-to-port drills:** maintain propulsion, steering, navigation, accommodation support, food, sanitation, medical care and communications while a main vertical zone is unavailable.
- **Crowd dynamics:** family separation, mobility-impaired passengers, multiple languages, aggressive behaviour, passenger refusal to comply and misinformation management.
- **Command decision exercises:** tabletop and simulator practice for the master and emergency-management team to decide between containment/return to port and abandonment.
- **Realistic evacuation drills:** include darkness, restricted routes, simulated injured persons and timed muster/embarkation—but without creating unsafe drill conditions.
- **Shore-side integration:** company emergency response, port authorities, flag State, coastal State, SAR services, hospitals and salvage support.

## Lessons from major casualties

| Casualty | Principal safety lesson | Examples of resulting regulatory or safety changes |
|---|---|---|
| **Titanic, 1912** | Lifeboat capacity, distress communications and ice-navigation precautions were fundamentally inadequate | The first SOLAS Convention followed in 1914; its core themes included sufficient lifesaving appliances, continuous radio watch and ice patrol arrangements. Modern SOLAS remains the principal international safety treaty.  [imo](https://www.imo.org/en/about/conventions/pages/international-convention-for-the-safety-of-life-at-sea-(solas),-1974.aspx) |
| **Herald of Free Enterprise, 1987** | Ro-ro vessels are highly vulnerable to water entering an open vehicle deck; organizational and operational failures can defeat equipment safeguards | Ro-ro-specific SOLAS measures were strengthened, including improved indicators/monitoring and operational requirements for bow, stern and side openings, plus enhanced damage-stability requirements. IMO notes that related amendments entered into force on 29 April 1990.  [wwwcdn.imo](https://wwwcdn.imo.org/localresources/en/OurWork/Safety/Documents/RORO.pdf) |
| **Scandinavian Star, 1990** | Passenger-ship fires require early detection, containment, smoke control, trained crews and safe escape | Strengthened fire-safety philosophy later consolidated in SOLAS II-2: prevent ignition and growth, control smoke, detect, contain and extinguish fire, maintain structural integrity and provide safe escape and training/drills.  [imo](https://www.imo.org/en/ourwork/safety/pages/summaryofsolaschapterii-2-default.aspx) |
| **Estonia, 1994** | Failure of bow-visors/ramps and subsequent vehicle-deck flooding can cause extremely rapid loss of stability | Strengthened ro-ro passenger-ship stability measures, bow-door/locking and monitoring requirements, and regional residual-stability measures such as the Stockholm Agreement framework. |

The common lesson is that passenger safety cannot be treated only as hull strength or only as lifeboat capacity. It is a combined problem of **design, watertight integrity, fire safety, human behaviour, training, command, maintenance and shore support**.

## Safe Return to Port

### Why amendments appear in SOLAS II-1 and II-2

They are split because safe return to port must address both **flooding survivability** and **fire survivability**.

| SOLAS chapter | Main casualty concern | Role in Safe Return to Port |
|---|---|---|
| **Chapter II-1** | Subdivision, stability, machinery and electrical installations; especially flooding | Requires the ship to retain required capability after a flooding casualty within the defined damage threshold, including assessment of flooding, compartment integrity and essential systems. |
| **Chapter II-2** | Fire protection, detection, containment and extinction | Requires the vessel to retain essential systems outside the fire-affected main vertical zone, provide safe areas and support orderly evacuation/abandonment if the fire casualty exceeds the threshold.  [imo](https://www.imo.org/en/ourwork/safety/pages/summaryofsolaschapterii-2-default.aspx) |

A collision may flood machinery spaces, electrical rooms or compartments and threaten stability; a fire may disable an entire main vertical zone, fill escape routes with smoke, or sever cables and pipework. Therefore, a single chapter could not adequately regulate both failure modes.

### Accident categories

For this subject, accident categories may be expressed as:

- **Flooding casualty:** collision, grounding, hull breach, pipe rupture, shell-door failure, progressive compartment flooding.
- **Fire or explosion casualty:** machinery-space fire, cabin fire, galley fire, electrical fire, vehicle-deck fire, fire in an atrium or service space.
- **Loss of essential-function casualty:** blackout, propulsion failure, steering failure, loss of navigation, loss of firefighting water, loss of communications.
- **Evacuation/SAR casualty:** a situation in which the ship can no longer safely sustain people onboard and abandonment or rescue is required.

In design terms, fire and flooding are treated separately because their damage propagation mechanisms differ: fire is controlled by containment, detection, suppression and redundancy; flooding is controlled by subdivision, buoyancy, stability, pumping and watertight integrity.

### Casualty threshold

The **casualty threshold** is the maximum extent of damage, defined in the ship’s design basis, that the ship can sustain and still safely return to port. [imo](https://www.imo.org/en/ourwork/safety/pages/passengerships.aspx)

Below threshold:

\[
\text{Casualty} \leq \text{threshold}
\Rightarrow
\text{safe return to port under own propulsion}
\]

Above threshold:

\[
\text{Casualty} > \text{threshold}
\Rightarrow
\text{ship must support orderly evacuation and abandonment}
\]

This is not a claim that the ship can survive every imaginable accident. It is a defined design standard that gives the master, crew and passengers a more reliable margin for foreseeable major casualties.

### Essential systems for Safe Return

SOLAS II-2 Regulation 21 identifies systems expected to remain operational, in the unaffected part of the ship, following a fire casualty within the threshold. They include:

- Propulsion.
- Steering and steering control.
- Navigation systems.
- Fuel-oil filling, transfer and service systems.
- Internal communications among bridge, engine spaces, safety centre, fire/damage-control teams, passengers and muster stations.
- External communications.
- Fire main and fixed extinguishing systems.
- Fire and smoke detection.
- Bilge and ballast systems.
- Power-operated watertight and semi-watertight doors.
- Flooding detection.
- Systems supporting safe areas.
- Other Administration-required systems vital to damage control. [imorules](http://www.imorules.com/GUID-5A6A972E-F1BF-470E-9E64-BD99966E3E02.html)

The design implication is redundancy and physical separation. For example, duplicate power cables must not be routed through the same fire zone if one fire can disable both; duplicate propulsion or steering capability must have sufficiently independent supporting systems.

### Essential services in safe areas

A **safe area** is a protected onboard space where passengers and crew can be accommodated during return to port after a casualty within the threshold. It must provide:

- Sanitation.
- Drinking water.
- Food.
- Alternative medical-care space.
- Shelter from weather.
- Measures to avoid hypothermia or heat stress.
- Lighting.
- Ventilation. [imorules](http://www.imorules.com/GUID-5A6A972E-F1BF-470E-9E64-BD99966E3E02.html)

For a prolonged return-to-port situation, this is a practical humanitarian requirement, not a theoretical one. People cannot safely remain aboard for many hours without toilets, water, food, basic medical treatment, heat/cooling, light and air.

### SAR in remote areas

In remote areas, immediate rescue may be unavailable because of distance, weather, lack of helicopters, few suitable vessels, weak communications or limited medical facilities ashore. A successful abandonment may still leave hundreds or thousands of persons in survival craft awaiting recovery.

IMO therefore developed:

- Guidance on external support to assist incident containment.
- Enhanced contingency-planning guidance for passenger ships in remote SAR areas.
- Recovery-technique guidance.
- Training guidance for SAR personnel in major incidents.
- Cold-water survival guidance.
- Voyage-planning guidance for passenger ships operating in remote areas. [imo](https://www.imo.org/en/ourwork/safety/pages/passengerships.aspx)

The operational implication is straightforward: before entering remote waters, the company and master should identify likely rescue coordination centres, nearby vessels, diversion ports, helicopter range, medical resources, communication contingencies and expected recovery times.

## Manila Statement and ferries

The **Manila Statement on the Enhancement of the Safety of Ships Carrying Passengers on Non-International Voyages** was adopted in Manila on 24 April 2015. It recognized continuing unacceptable loss of life in domestic-ferry accidents and emphasized that domestic ferries often fall outside international SOLAS application even though sea hazards do not distinguish between international and domestic services. [wwwcdn.imo](https://wwwcdn.imo.org/localresources/en/OurWork/Safety/Documents/Manila%20Statement.pdf)

### Adequacy of the initiative

The initiative is valuable because it takes a **fit-for-purpose, risk-based** approach rather than attempting to impose every international SOLAS requirement unchanged on small, short-route domestic ferries.

It calls for national systems that address:

- Vessel design, stability, freeboard, watertight integrity and structural strength.
- Safe conversion or modification of second-hand vessels.
- Reassessment when a ferry changes route, operating area or service limits.
- Passenger counting and reliable records of all persons on board.
- Proper voyage planning, weather information and navigational support.
- Adequate crew competency, survey, inspection, certification and enforcement.
- Fire detection, alarms, escape routes, lifesaving appliances, bilge arrangements and ro-ro deck drainage.
- Investigation of casualties and implementation of lessons learned. [wwwcdn.imo](https://wwwcdn.imo.org/localresources/en/OurWork/Safety/Documents/Manila%20Statement.pdf)

### Core regional problems

The Statement identifies recurring conditions that make coastal and inland ferry safety difficult, particularly in developing regions:

- Fares may be too low to make services commercially viable without compromising maintenance, crew standards or vessel quality.
- Old second-hand ships may be purchased for routes and weather conditions for which they were never designed.
- Vessels may be converted without adequate engineering assessment or stability re-evaluation.
- Ships may be moved from sheltered to exposed routes without reviewing service limits.
- Commercial or social pressure may lead to passenger overloading.
- Regulators may lack sufficiently trained surveyors, auditors, inspectors and examiners.
- Weather forecasting, aids to navigation, radio communications and SAR infrastructure may be limited. [wwwcdn.imo](https://wwwcdn.imo.org/localresources/en/OurWork/Safety/Documents/Manila%20Statement.pdf)

### Evaluation

The Manila approach is **adequate in principle**, because it recognizes that domestic operations need scalable, locally enforceable standards and promotes eventual safety equivalence with SOLAS and STCW rather than superficial paper compliance.

Its limitation is implementation. Guidance alone cannot prevent casualties unless States provide:

- Effective legislation and independent enforcement.
- Reliable passenger-counting controls.
- Competent marine survey and inspection.
- Route-specific design and weather limitations.
- Training and certification systems for masters and crews.
- Enforcement against overloading and unsafe conversions.
- Investment in navigation aids, communications, emergency response and SAR.

A strong exam conclusion is:

> The Manila Statement correctly treats domestic ferry safety as a shared responsibility of government, owners, operators, shipboard staff, regulators, ports, classification bodies, insurers and passengers. Its success depends not on the existence of guidelines, but on practical enforcement, competent people and vessels demonstrably suited to their route, weather exposure and passenger load. [wwwcdn.imo](https://wwwcdn.imo.org/localresources/en/OurWork/Safety/Documents/Manila%20Statement.pdf)
